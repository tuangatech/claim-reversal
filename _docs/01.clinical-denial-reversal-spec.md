# Clinical Denial Reversal Workflow Agent
## Project Specification v0.2

---

## 1. Background

When a hospital submits a claim to a payer, the payer may deny it on **clinical grounds** — meaning they disagree that the service was medically necessary, appropriate, or covered under the patient's plan. These denials are not billing errors; they are clinical judgments, and reversing them requires building a clinical argument supported by documentation.

Clinical denials are among the most labor-intensive to reverse. They require a coordinated effort between billing staff, clinical staff, and sometimes legal or external reviewers. Most hospitals lack the bandwidth to work these systematically, which is where automation and intelligent workflow management can make a real difference.

Much of the denial reversal process is deterministic — parsing reason codes, checking deadlines, routing by priority, logging submissions. A rules-based application handles these steps well. The hard parts are evaluating whether unstructured clinical records demonstrate medical necessity against payer-specific criteria, and drafting a case-specific argument that refutes the payer's rationale. These require judgment over ambiguous, unstructured content — the work that burns the most human hours and determines whether appeals succeed or fail.

> **Scope**: Side/learning project. Workflows are simplified representations of real RCM operations.
> The goal is to model the business logic faithfully enough to build meaningful agents, not to replace a production system.

---

## 2. Key Actors

| Actor | Role |
|---|---|
| **Billing Specialist** | Receives and triages the denial; initiates the appeal process; submits the final appeal |
| **Clinical Reviewer** | Pulls relevant patient records and clinical notes; validates medical necessity |
| **Appeal Writer** | Assembles the formal appeal letter using denial context and clinical evidence |
| **Medical Director / Physician Advisor** | Reviews and signs off on clinical appeals that require physician attestation |
| **Payer** | Receives the appeal (response comes weeks later — outside this workflow) |

---

## 3. Trigger

The workflow begins when a **claim denial arrives** from the payer — typically as an Explanation of Benefits (EOB) or a remittance file (835 EDI). The denial includes:

- The **claim ID** and patient information
- The **denial reason code** (e.g., CO-50: "Not medically necessary")
- The **payer's clinical rationale**, if provided
- The **appeal deadline** — usually 30 to 180 days depending on payer and plan type

---

## 4. Workflow

### Step 1 — Denial Intake & Triage

**Who**: Billing Specialist

The billing team receives the denial and logs it. They check:
- What was denied and why (denial reason code)
- Whether the denial is clinical (not medically necessary, experimental, level-of-care mismatch) vs. administrative (missing auth, wrong code)
- Whether the appeal window is still open
- Whether this claim is worth appealing based on dollar amount and complexity

The "worth appealing" decision uses a simple rule: **proceed if the claim balance ≥ $1,000 and the denial reason code is CO-50 ("not medically necessary") or CO-57 ("experimental/investigational")**. Additionally, **prior appeal history penalizes the worthiness score** — if a prior appeal for the same denial reason was already rejected, the claim is less likely to proceed. Claims below the threshold, with non-qualifying denial codes, or with unfavorable prior appeal history are logged and closed without appeal. This is a simplified heuristic — real organizations use more nuanced scoring — but it gives the agent a testable decision boundary.

**Output**: A denial record with classification (clinical vs. administrative), priority, and deadline. Only clinical denials proceed through this workflow.

---

### Step 2 — Clinical Evidence Gathering

**Who**: Clinical Reviewer (with support from billing)

The clinical reviewer pulls together the documentation needed to support the appeal:
- Patient medical records relevant to the date of service
- Physician orders and progress notes
- Discharge summary (for inpatient claims)
- Lab results, imaging reports, or other diagnostic evidence
- Any prior authorization approvals on file

They also look up the **clinical criteria the payer used** to deny the claim — for example, InterQual or MCG guidelines — and identify where the patient's case meets those criteria.

**Output**: A clinical evidence bundle: the relevant records plus a summary of how the patient's condition meets medical necessity criteria.

---

### Step 3 — Appeal Strategy Selection

**Who**: Billing Specialist + Clinical Reviewer

Based on the denial reason and the evidence gathered, the team decides on an approach:

- **First-level appeal**: Standard written appeal to the payer with clinical documentation. Used for most denials.
- **Peer-to-peer review**: The treating physician speaks directly with the payer's medical director. Used when the clinical case is strong but nuanced, or when a written appeal alone is unlikely to succeed.

> **Descoped**: Expedited appeals (fast-track for patient harm risk) are a real-world strategy but are excluded from this project. They would require different SLAs, accelerated evidence gathering, and submission deadlines that add complexity without teaching new agent patterns. All appeals in this workflow follow the standard timeline.

**Output**: Chosen appeal strategy and assignment of who will draft and sign the appeal.

---

### Step 4 — Appeal Letter Drafting

**Who**: Appeal Writer (billing specialist or dedicated appeals staff)

The appeal letter is assembled using:
- The denial details and payer's stated rationale
- The clinical evidence bundle from Step 2
- Citations to the relevant clinical guidelines (InterQual, MCG, or payer-specific criteria)
- A clear narrative arguing why the service was medically necessary

The letter follows the payer's required format and submission method (mail, fax, or payer portal).

**Output**: A completed appeal letter with supporting attachments, ready for physician review.

---

### Step 5 — Physician Sign-Off (if required)

**Who**: Medical Director or Physician Advisor

For appeals that require physician attestation — or where the clinical argument needs to be signed by a licensed physician — the appeal is routed for review and signature before submission.

This step may be bypassed for lower-complexity appeals that billing staff can submit independently.

**Output**: Signed appeal letter or peer-to-peer review request.

---

### Step 6 — Appeal Submission

**Who**: Billing Specialist

The appeal is submitted to the payer through the required channel within the deadline. A submission record is created with the date, method, and confirmation number (if available).

**Output**: Submission confirmation with date, method, and confirmation number. The workflow ends here — payer response typically takes 30–60 days (commercial) or longer (Medicare/Medicaid), which is outside the scope of this synchronous workflow.

> **Out of scope**: Outcome monitoring, escalation, and second-level appeals are real-world follow-up processes that happen weeks later. They would be modeled as a separate async workflow triggered by incoming remittance files — not as a continuation of the submission workflow.

## 5. Decision Points Summary

```
Denial Received
      │
      ▼
Is it a clinical denial?
  No  → Exit (route to different workflow)
  Yes ↓
Is the appeal window open?
  No  → Log and close
  Yes ↓
Is the claim worth appealing?
  No  → Log and close
  Yes ↓
Gather clinical evidence
      │
      ▼
Is evidence sufficient?
  No  → Request additional records (loop back, max 2 retries)
  Still insufficient after retries → ⚠️ HUMAN DECISION REQUIRED
      Human chooses: Proceed with available evidence | Close case
  Yes ↓
Draft appeal letter
      │
      ▼
Physician sign-off required?
  Yes → Route for review and signature
  No  → Continue
      │
      ▼
Submit appeal → END
  (Payer response arrives weeks later — separate workflow)
```

## 6. Key Data Elements

The following pieces of information flow through the entire workflow:

- **Claim ID** — unique identifier tying everything together
- **Patient info** — demographics, insurance details
- **Date of service** — determines applicable payer rules and guidelines
- **Denial reason code** — drives the appeal strategy
- **Appeal deadline** — hard constraint the whole workflow must respect
- **Clinical evidence bundle** — records, notes, lab results
- **Guideline citation** — which criteria were used and how the patient meets them
- **Appeal letter** — the primary artifact of the workflow
- **Submission confirmation** — proof of timely filing (workflow terminal artifact)

## 7. Out of Scope (for this project)

- Real payer portal submission
- HIPAA-compliant data handling

All clinical data, payer responses, and guidelines will be **mocked or LLM-generated** for learning purposes.

## 8. Human-in-the-Loop (HITL)

### When and Why

The workflow includes one human decision point: when the Clinical Evidence Agent exhausts its retry attempts and still cannot confirm that the gathered evidence is sufficient to support the appeal. At this point the system cannot make a good decision autonomously — proceeding with weak evidence wastes everyone's time; silently abandoning the claim loses recoverable revenue. A human reviewer must decide.

The retry cap is **2 attempts** — an arbitrary but practical default for a learning project. In production, retry behavior would vary by denial type, appeal urgency, and remaining time before the filing deadline. For this project, 2 retries provides enough cycles to demonstrate the LangGraph loop + HITL escalation pattern without over-complicating the graph.

### What the Human Sees

The workflow pauses and presents the reviewer with:
- The denial reason and payer's stated rationale
- The evidence gathered so far (records found, guideline criteria retrieved)
- The agent's explanation of *why* the evidence is insufficient
- Two choices: **Proceed** (file the appeal with what we have) or **Close** (abandon this appeal)

### What Happens Next

- **Proceed**: The workflow resumes with the available (incomplete) evidence bundle. The appeal letter will note that full documentation was not available. This is a legitimate real-world scenario.
- **Close**: The claim is logged as closed with reason "insufficient evidence after review." No appeal is filed.

### Scope Boundary

This is the **only** HITL point in the project. Physician sign-off is simulated automatically to keep scope manageable.

## 9. Success Criteria (Learning Goals)

This project is considered successful if it demonstrates:

1. A multi-agent system where each agent has a clear, bounded responsibility
2. LangGraph conditional branching that reflects real decision logic (sufficient evidence? retry or escalate to human?)
3. At least one remote A2A agent call (Clinical Evidence Agent as a remote service)
4. At least one shared MCP tool used by multiple agents (audit log, guideline lookup)
5. A human-in-the-loop pause at evidence insufficiency — workflow suspends, human decides, workflow resumes
6. A Playwright-based tool that navigates a website to retrieve policy documents (stretch goal)
7. Mock data that is realistic enough to make the agents' reasoning meaningful
